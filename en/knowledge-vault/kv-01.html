Why Would a Relationship Manager (RM) Hand Over What's in Their Head?

Nov 17, 20266 min read

Almost every time we talk about knowledge vaults, a business head asks this question, and it is the right question to ask. My answer: let RMs enjoy the benefits themselves first, and the knowledge gets captured along the way. Only once that step is in place is there a chance of asking RMs to share their real experience.

A large part of an RM’s value sits in their head: the client’s context, the history of the relationship, and the reasons behind each recommendation. Asking them to hand it over is asking them to put part of their livelihood on the table. This is not RMs being stingy. Behavioural economics calls it the endowment effect: once something is ours, we are reluctant to give it up. In the well-known “mug experiment”, people who were given a mug asked roughly twice as much to sell it as people without one were willing to pay.

When RMs hand over knowledge, they do not know any less than before; what they lose is being “the only one who knows”. That registers as a loss, and a loss hurts much more than a gain of the same size. So this is a question of how incentives are designed, not a question of character. No amount of “sharing is a virtue” will help.

Our view

Rules alone will not work; you need a tool people want to use. Rules can cover the records that must be kept, but not the judgement in people’s heads. Announce a policy that says “please put your knowledge into the system”, and most of what you get back is content written to tick a box. Linking the number of contributions to KPIs is no different: it measures quantity, not quality. What works is first giving RMs a tool they want to use for themselves: one that answers questions on the spot, prepares material quickly before meetings, and writes meeting notes back into the CRM automatically.

“Knowledge is captured as a by-product of doing the work, not as extra homework.”

There are two reasons for this design. First, the cost of entering knowledge is paid now, while the benefit comes later and mostly goes to someone else, so RMs need to get the benefit on the same day. Second, as Stanford’s BJ Fogg has put it, for a behaviour to happen, motivation, ability and a prompt all have to be present at the same time; if one is missing, it does not happen. Campaigns and pep talks add motivation, but what usually gets in the way is ability: another log-in, more typing, more time. Removing that barrier does more than another briefing session.

“Captured along the way” already has an industry precedent, but it only proves half the case. Morgan Stanley’s Debrief records meetings with the client’s consent, produces a summary and action items, and saves the notes to the CRM. Emails to clients are sent only after the adviser has reviewed them. What this proves is that RMs are willing to use the tool, not that they are willing to hand over knowledge. The tool can keep what was said in the meeting; why the adviser made that call, or how to respond to a difficult client, is not in the notes. From the public information I have seen, no bank has yet proven it can do that second half. So the three points below are judgements, not a tested formula.

Give RMs a role before asking for their experience. For RMs to open up, you first have to answer the question on their mind: once I have taught the AI, what is left for me? Handing over knowledge is seen as a loss; if what they hand over could then be used to replace them, the loss only gets bigger. So first make clear what their role will be: moving from doing the work by hand to improving, testing and supervising the AI. Leave the decision with them too: they choose which experience to share with everyone, rather than the system taking everything. People only commit when they feel the decision is their own.

Making people feel safe to write matters more than pushing them to write. Once RMs are willing to share, there is another concern that people rarely say out loud: once you write something down, you are accountable for it. If it stays in your head and you are wrong, nobody knows; once it is in the system, it may be held up for review one day. Afraid of regretting it later, people simply do not write. So there needs to be a drafts area, review in layers, and a way to withdraw something written in error.

Valuable know-how can sit behind a threshold; compliance content cannot. Once people feel safe to write, they need a reason to keep writing. A design where “you see others’ best material only if you contribute” suits top performers’ sales experience, closing techniques and practical methods. Keep the threshold low: a newcomer asking a good question should count as a contribution, or the people who most need the best material will be the ones who cannot see it. Product risk, suitability, compliance alerts and approved investment views must be visible to everyone, with no threshold. If you use points to keep people away from compliance content, it will be very hard to explain to clients and regulators when something goes wrong.

Where to start

Starting with “please, everyone, enter more” will probably run into trouble; we suggest two steps. Step one tests only use: pick a narrow topic whose content should be visible to everyone anyway, such as Q&A on approved investment views, and run it for 90 days. Record today’s numbers as a baseline, then measure one thing: are RMs really using it every week? Step two tests handovers: how many fewer questions does the person taking over need to ask? Choosing approved content has another advantage: someone already checks the answers. Once an RM has been burned by a wrong answer, they usually never open the tool again; there is no second chance at a first impression. There is also no need to survey RMs on whether they are “willing to share”; questions like that only get answers that sound good.

Whether RMs open the tool every week tells you far more than anything they say.
