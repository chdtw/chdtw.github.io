Four Signs That Show Whether Your Knowledge Governance Is AI-Ready

Nov 18, 20266 min read

  • Knowledge Vault
  • Gen AI

Before scaling up the use of AI, we listen for whether anyone in a meeting mentions these four things. If two or more come up, we suggest putting some measures in place before scaling up. The four reinforce one another, and AI carries them straight to the end client.

Before AI went live, a financial institution’s internal knowledge was always being managed; it was just mostly managed in people’s heads. Experienced colleagues know which document is out of date, whom to ask about what, and which rule has exceptions. That works well enough day to day, because people stop and ask when a document looks wrong. AI does not stop and ask. It answers far more questions than any single colleague, and it quotes whatever it is given. The gaps that people used to fill all show up at once.

The four sentences below are the ones I hear most often inside banks. Behind each sign is an underlying issue, and each comes with a check you can run internally.

Sign 1: “You’ll have to ask them. Only they know.”

The underlying issue: knowledge is scattered and not connected. The rules are in compliance announcements, the process is in the operations manual, how to handle exceptions is in experienced colleagues’ heads, and the validation rules in the system are yet another set. Every piece exists; they are just not connected.

Day to day, this sentence sounds like praise for that colleague. Heard another way, it says that the last piece of the judgement is not written down anywhere. Knowledge management research calls this kind of “able to judge, but unable to explain the basis” knowledge tacit knowledge. AI can read the announcements and the manuals, but not the part that lives in colleagues’ heads. So the answers AI gives look complete, but the exceptions are missing.

Check for yourself: pick a decision that comes up often, such as “can this client buy this product?”. Count how many people you have to ask and how many systems and documents you have to check to put together a complete answer. If any step is “ask a particular person”, this sign applies.

Sign 2: “Our part stops here. The rest isn’t ours.”

The underlying issue: each unit owns a small segment, and nobody owns the whole chain. The product unit owns product documents, compliance owns the rules, and IT owns the system rules. Every segment has an owner, but when a decision runs from start to finish, nobody looks at the handovers in between.

Nobody is failing at their job. That is simply how the work was divided, and each segment is done well. The trouble is that AI’s answers run across the segments: one answer draws on product documents, rules and system rules all at once. When the answer is wrong, the three units each check their own part, and none of them finds an error.

Check for yourself: take the same decision and ask, “From the data to the final decision, who is responsible for the whole chain?” If you get one person’s name, this item passes. If you get the names of three units, or silence, this sign applies.

Sign 3: “I found the document, but I’m not sure it can still be used.”

The underlying issue: you can get hold of content without knowing whether you may use it, or what happens if you do. Which clients a piece of content applies to, how long it is valid, what it may be used for and who is authorised to use it are mostly not written down.

The colleague who says this has actually done one thing right: they hesitated. People hesitate; AI does not, it quotes directly. So making search more precise does not solve this one. An industry guide on enterprise knowledge bases puts it bluntly: the retrieval pipeline is fine; what is unfit is the document it retrieves.

Check for yourself: open any internal document. Looking only at the document itself, try to find whom it applies to, its validity period and its usage restrictions. If you have to phone the author to find out, it counts.

Sign 4: “The rules have changed. What else has to change with them? Let’s email around first.”

The underlying issue: nobody can say for sure what a change affects. After a policy or product terms are updated, the affected processes, scripts, validation rules and training materials are spread all over the place, and people have to remember them one by one.

When only people are using the content, emailing around is just about workable; whatever gets missed will be found sooner or later. Once AI is connected, it is different: in the days between the announcement and the last update, every time AI is asked, it answers from the old content, and with full confidence.

Check for yourself: think back to the most recent change in a policy or in product terms. How long did it take from the announcement until you were sure every affected place had been updated, and was anything found only afterwards? If you cannot say how long it took, or something was found afterwards, this sign applies.

How to read the result

If two or more of the four signs apply, we suggest not rushing to scale up AI, because the four signs reinforce one another: if knowledge is not connected, you cannot see what a change affects; if nobody can look at the whole process end to end, nobody defines which content applies in which situation. Fix only one sign, and the others will pull it back to where it started.

These four signs are only a first, quick diagnosis. Each sign is handled differently, and the order in which to tackle them depends on how urgent the actual business needs are.
