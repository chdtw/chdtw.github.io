Refining, the Step After a Document Comes In: How a Knowledge Vault Differs from a Traditional Knowledge Base (Part 1)

Nov 19, 20265 min read

  • Knowledge Vault
  • Gen AI

“We already have a document-based knowledge base (KB). How is a knowledge vault (KV) different?” Clients often ask us this. KBs and KVs differ in several ways. In this article we use the step that comes after a document is added, which we call “refining”, to explain the difference.

In short: a document-based KB takes in a document, builds the index, and the import is done. A KV goes further: it breaks the new content into knowledge units that can each be understood on their own (deconstruct), compares them with related knowledge already in the vault (cross-check), identifies clearly which existing knowledge they add to, correct or challenge, or what new topics they raise (position), and then writes the result, together with its sources, back into existing or new knowledge pages (integrate). We call this process “refining”.

A case to show the difference between a KB and a KV

Suppose we have collected 10 books on running and managing a business. We give the same ten books to a KB and to a KV, and see how each handles them.

How a KB handles them: someone has to search first, and then it returns documents

With a traditional document-based KB, once the ten books are added and the index is updated, the job is done; from then on it waits for someone to search.

Say we have a particular topic in mind, for example how these books discuss “performance appraisal”. The KB uses its index to find the books that touch on the topic and lists them. From there we are on our own: we have to pull out those books, read them, compare their views and work out our own conclusions.

That work, however, stays only in one person’s head or notes. The next person who looks into the same topic has to repeat everything: go back to the same books and read them again, and the two people may not reach the same conclusions. How the first person put those passages together and synthesised them is not kept in the KB.

What is more, if there is no particular topic and nobody searches, the KB does no organising in advance. In other words, the ability to “connect and make sense of” these 10 books depends entirely on the limits of the human brain. Put another way, a KB is like an online library.

How a KV handles them: knowledge is organised as soon as the books come in

Give the same ten books to a KV, and the work starts when they are added. It breaks each book into individual pieces of content and compares them with what is already in the vault. For example, one book describes a company that does away with performance appraisal altogether; another warns that adding more and more metrics only makes an organisation more complex; a third argues that if you hire strictly enough, managing people later becomes easy. After comparing them, the KV recognises that these three books are talking about the same thing: “performance evaluation systems”. So the KV creates a new knowledge page on its own initiative, “Performance evaluation systems”, covering which questions each book answers, where their views differ and which chapter each view comes from.

Note that at this point nobody has asked about performance appraisal yet. The person who added the books did not even need to know it was a question worth asking; they simply added ten books. If someone later happens to ask about performance evaluation systems, the KV can answer directly from this page. The next person who asks the same question also starts from this page. Or people can open this knowledge page and read it in full, and even follow the links in each paragraph straight back to the source books.

And if an 11th book comes in later, the KV checks whether it contains anything on performance evaluation. If it does, the KV compares it with the existing knowledge, checks the same knowledge page and works out what needs to change.
