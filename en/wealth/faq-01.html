Complex Sources of Wealth Can't Be Standardised, So How Could AI Judge Them?

Nov 15, 20265 min read

  • Private Banking
  • Wealth Management

Private banking clients do not come with a fixed checklist of proof of wealth. People who got rich from crypto, professional athletes, founders who sold their companies: every background is different. So source of wealth (SoW) is often raised as a counter-argument: if even people have to rely on experience to judge it, how could AI? My answer is that nobody can standardise every case, but the logic of the judgement can be standardised.

A word of caution first

There really is no universal checklist. For cases involving private companies, artworks or land, deciding what proof to ask for takes an experienced person’s judgement. AI cannot tell whether a document is genuine or whether a person can be trusted. What AI can do is organise traceable evidence into risk signals and offer how similar cases were handled in practice; in the end, a person reviews it and a person is accountable.

But the real problem is not the handful of hardest cases. High-risk cases already have a review path; inconsistent standards actually happen in the high-volume, low-to-medium-risk cases, where the same kind of source of wealth gets different evidence requests from different case handlers. When a relationship manager (RM) meets a client with an unusual background and does not know which documents to ask for, they submit the case anyway. Compliance sends it back as “insufficient documentation” but cannot say exactly what is needed. After a few rounds, each side thinks the problem lies with the other. The root cause is that nobody has that checklist.

Our view

What gets standardised is the logic of the judgement, not the case. First break sources of wealth into types that can be listed, and under each type record four things: what the usual documents are, what alternative evidence there is when the main document cannot be produced, what level of evidence counts as sufficient, and what kind of evidence is itself a red flag. Singapore’s AML/CFT Industry Partnership (ACIP) wrote its May 2025 best practices for source-of-wealth due diligence in exactly this way: one chapter each for inheritance and gifts, business ownership, investment income, sale of hard-to-value assets, and employment income, each setting out supporting documents and what to do when they cannot be obtained, followed by case examples. The checklist is a starting point, not a ceiling; the “red flag” column is where the case handler is forced to stop and think.

How deep to dig also follows a method. Every extra round of document requests should answer two questions: which claim is this document meant to verify? And once we have it, could it change the conclusion? If it could, follow up; if not, stop. The Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) circular to the wealth management industry in May 2026 makes exactly this point: allocate the depth of verification according to risk and materiality. What gets cut is document requests with no reason behind them, not the rigour of the review.

What AI can judge is completeness and consistency. Once the first two are written down, AI has something to work on: whether the documents are complete, whether they contradict one another, what is missing, and which requirement has not been met. There are three positions where it can help. At intake, it tells the RM which documents to collect, what is still missing and where the documents provided conflict with one another, so that rejections are dealt with before the case is submitted. It produces source-of-wealth write-ups in a consistent format, setting the amount the client claims against the amount the evidence can support; the gap itself is a signal. And it finds similar cases from the past and how they were decided. In October 2025, Bank of Singapore announced that it had introduced AI to write source-of-wealth reports, and said the time had dropped from about ten days to about one hour. What was shortened was the collating and writing; the judgement stays with people.

The criteria need a place to live, and a named person to approve them. All three positions above have AI reading the same layer: the types, the evidence requirements, the depth of verification, and how cases were decided in the past. What we see is that this layer mostly does not exist. Judgements are scattered across emails between RMs and compliance, and the reason for a rejection is reduced to “insufficient documentation”, with nothing left for the next person to use. In the criteria layer, every criterion should carry its basis, version, review date and owner. That way every case handler uses the same yardstick, and when auditors ask about a case from six months ago, the answer changes from “trust us, we reviewed it” to “you can see which criterion it was assessed against, what evidence was examined and who approved it”. AI can propose candidate criteria, but what becomes the bank’s official standard is approved by a named person.

Where to start

Start with the three highest-volume types of source of wealth, and pull out the past year’s approved and rejected cases. Every approval and every rejection is a judgement of the form “so this is how this kind of situation has to be proven”. Put those judgements together and you have the first version of the baseline.

Change one thing while you are at it: from the next rejection onwards, state clearly which type of source of wealth it is, which kind of evidence is missing, and what needs to be added for it to be enough.
